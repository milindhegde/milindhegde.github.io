Student Probability Seminar, Fall 2018

Wednesday 2-3 PM, 939 Evans

This is the UC Berkeley Student Probability Seminar, run by students in both the Mathematics and Statistics Departments at UC Berkeley. We meet on Wednesdays in 939 Evans from 2-3pm, right before the probability seminar.

This semester we will be reading Large Deviations: Techniques and Applications by Dembo and Zeitouni. The book is available for free (on campus through Springer Link) here. We will also have talks on other probability topics every few weeks.

Title and Abstracts

Large deviation theory setup - Chapters 1 and 2.1

Efe Aras

We are going to do an introduction on Large Deviations; we will first motivate the setup and then establish the framework that will be used throughout the semester. We then will consider convergence of empirical laws on finite alphabets in this setup. While doing so, we will discuss basic information theoretic quantities and see how they show up in Sanov's theorem.

Cramer's theorem on finite alphabets: 2.1.2 - 2.1.3

Ella Hiesmayr

We will first see Cramer's theorem for finite alphabets, which is an application of Sanov's Theorem to the sequence of empirical means. Then we will prove an asymptotic version of Sanov's Theorem, that can be obtained by sampling without replacement from sequences from a finite alphabet.

Cramer's Theorem on \(\mathbb R\) (upper bound): 2.2.1

Milind Hegde

We will review the significance of a "good" rate function and state the contraction principle and its relation to Cramer's theorem. Then we will develop some intuition for Cramer's theorem for iid random variables on \(\mathbb R\) and prove the upper bound. We will finally illustrate a simple heuristic reason for why, conditioned on the rare event in Cramer's theorem, the individual random variables behave iid with a different mean.

Cramer's Theorem on \(\mathbb R\) (lower bound): 2.2.1

Alexander Tsigler

We will finish the proof of Cramer's theorem on \(\mathbb{R}\) by showing the lower bound.

Large Deviations in Economics

Farzad Pourbabaee

I will first present a paper on Bayesian decision theory that uses the tools from theory of large deviation to formalize the value of information acquisition. The paper pins down the marginal value of information, and then proposes an information demand function in terms of sample-price. If time permits, I present a second paper that focuses on the learning dynamics of the Rational Expectation Equilibria (REE). Using the large deviation theory it is verified how the learning process could escape from REE path, that explains why sometimes asset-prices deviate from their rational value and exhibit bubbles. Knowledge of economics is not required!

Links to the papers: Paper 1, Paper 2

Hydrodynamics of the inhomogeneous \(\ell\)-TASEP

Dan Daniel Erdmann-Pham

The totally asymmetric simple exclusion process is an interacting particle system modelling the stochastic, unidirectional traffic of particles along a lattice. Here we will introduce its cousin, the open boundary inhomogeneous \(\ell\)-TASEP, starting from its motivations in biology, touching on exact solutions of special cases, and ending with a high level overview of its hydrodynamic theory. If time permits, we will revisit its biological origins and explore what implications the hydrodynamic limit may have for applications.

Cramer's Theorem on \(\mathbb R^d\) and the Gartner-Ellis Theorem: 2.2.2

Yiling You

We will proceed to prove the Cramer’s theorem in \(\mathbb R^d\).

Gärtner-Ellis Theorem: 2.3

Zsolt Bartha

We will generalize Cramér's Theorem going beyond the empirical mean of iid's case by proving the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem, using only the properties of the moment generating functions. We will see an important application where the dependence is described by a Markov chain.

Inequalities for Bounded Martingale Differences: 2.4.1

Zhiyi You

We will first investigate concentration properties of discrete time martingales of bounded differences. Then we will show how these may apply for certain functionals of independent variables.

Schramm-Loewner Evolutions - I

Ian Francis

We will discuss the Schramm-Loewner evolution (SLE), a fundamental construction in probability theory used to describe scaling limits of discrete models from statistical mechanics. We will first review some preliminary tools needed to define SLE and then discuss some properties of SLE for different values of kappa (phases, scaling limit of percolation, self-avoiding walks, etc.). No prior knowledge of SLE is needed, but some familiarity with stochastic calculus will be useful. The content of these talks is based off the lecture notes of Jason Miller and Nathanaël Berestycki.
